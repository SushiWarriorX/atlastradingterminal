# Running an experiment you can trust

The aim is not to find a winner; it is to find out, cheaply and honestly, whether an idea has an edge. A backtest that was shaped after seeing its result proves nothing.

## Before you run anything
1. **Write the rule with every number in it** (windows, thresholds, costs, caps, data, universe, the date split). Put it in `atlas/EDGE_PLAN.md` under a heading that says "pre-registered" and the time.
2. **Write the pass bar in the same place.** Use conditions that can be checked with numbers and that include a fair comparison: against simply holding, and against the simplest version of the idea (for example "hold the same average amount", "hold the equal-weighted list"). An idea that only beats a straw man has not passed.
3. **Commit and push that file first.** The commit time is the evidence that the bar was set before the result.
4. **Name the known biases** you cannot remove (for example "the coin list is made of coins listed today, so survivors only") and decide in advance how the result will be worded because of them.
5. **Commit the runner and its tests before the first run**, and write any clarification of the wording (what counts as a switch, which weeks of data are "used", how a shuffle is built) into the plan file first. Looking at the DATA's ranges, gaps and units before the run is allowed; looking at returns or any strategy number is not. Say in the log that the data look happened and that nothing was changed because of a result.

## Building the test
- Only use information available at the decision time. Decide at a close, trade at the next open, charge fees and slippage on the amount traded.
- Seal the last 30% of the history. Judge on it. Do not look at it while designing.
- Validate the simulator itself with hand-worked numbers, a no-look-ahead test (a prefix of the series gives the same decisions), a costs test, and agreement with the existing engine in a case both can run.
- Do not fill gaps in the data; report them and make the rule handle them.
- Test the comparison machinery with a planted case and an empty one: data where the rule truly leads price must pass every condition, data where it does not must fail the luck check. Also test the paths that make a run INVALID (a hole in the inputs, a wrong unit): invalid is not a fail, and it prints no performance numbers.
- A random-timing check keeps what the rule holds and breaks only the alignment: shift the rule's own on/off schedule around a circle by whole weeks (same exposure, same weekday for every switch) and compare against many shifts. It separates timing skill from simply holding less in a bad stretch.
- Know exactly what the simulator trades: which weight is first used, whether the last one is ever traded, whether the entry from flat costs money, and test it with a flat price so only costs move the curve.

## Running it
- **Once.** No variants, no "just one more setting", no rescue. A different idea is a new experiment with its own pre-registration.
- Report the numbers for the sealed period and the whole history, and for every comparison named in the bar.

## After
- Say plainly pass or fail against the bar written down. A fail is a result: keep it in the findings log with its numbers.
- A pass is only a candidate. It still needs the library-wide luck check (more rules tried means a higher bar) and a forward record on new data before anyone relies on it.
- Every report states: what improved, what was verified, what was NOT verified, and whether anything passed. Never write that something "makes money".
- Count the cost of searching: each extra test of the same kind makes a lucky winner more likely. Prefer different kinds of ideas over variants of one.

## Lessons from experiments 4 and 5 (2026-11-14)
- **Check a result a second way before believing it, a NEGATIVE one most of all.** Recompute the headline number with a plain, independent method (for example close-to-close) and read what the rule did on a few well-known dates. A wiring bug can fake a failure as easily as a success.
- **Break your own checks on purpose.** Change a limit or skip a test, and confirm a test fails; a check nothing tests is decoration. Do this for every data check a loader makes.
- **When a test fails, find out whether the code or the test is wrong before touching either.** In both experiments the first failures were slips in the test's own arithmetic.
- **Finish the loop in the app.** Add the result to `btclab/experiments_ledger.json` so Signal's Experiments record shows it, update the counts the tests and the browser script expect, and read the screenshot of the newest entry.
- **A rate limit or a refusal is not a result.** Retry a rate limit with a pause (nothing changes in the rule); a host that refuses the client is BLOCKED and the experiment waits for the owner.

## Lessons from the btcquant protocol (2026-11-15)
- **Test every gate for feasibility before the first run.** For each pass bar, work out by hand whether the kind of strategy it is meant to judge could ever pass it. Three of mine could not: a "30 trades" admission gate against slow trend rules that make a handful of trades in five years; "10 percentage points shallower than the exposure-matched hold" against a 1%-risk strategy whose matched hold is itself under 10 points deep; and an account that halts for good after one 15% drawdown, run on a full-exposure Bitcoin strategy that trips within months. A gate nothing can pass turns "cannot be judged" into "no edge". Fix it in a dated, versioned amendment with the reasons, BEFORE any run and never after a result has been seen.
- **Report three outcomes, not two:** rejected (the evidence says no), admitted (worth watching), and too thin to judge (not enough trades or too short a record to say anything). Only the first is a statement about edge.
- **Compare strategies that are invested very differently on scale-free measures** (Sharpe ratio, a ratio of drawdowns), never on a difference in percentage points, and always beside a hold with the same average exposure.
- **A gate written for one kind of family can be unpassable for another.** The stability gate looks at neighbours in a parameter grid, so it failed every calendar rule by construction (a weekday has none), and the random-timing null contains near-copies of a weekly schedule. Before running a family of a new kind, work every gate through on paper, run the family alone on synthetic worlds with no effect and with a planted one (`calibration.run_family_world`), and write into its note the false-admission rate, the size of effect the procedure can see and what "rejected" will therefore mean. Do all of it before the run, in the same push as the pre-registration.
- **A source list is a list of reasons to test, not of results.** Write down which records were confirmed, which claims are only the authors' abstract, which families have no published support at all (say so in the note), and one source that argues against.

## Engineering traps when a family is added under a new protocol version (step 14b, 2026-11-15)
- **Do the version bump and the new family in one change, then run every btcquant test and expect a short list to fail.** Since step 14c the tests compare with `C.PROTOCOL_VERSION` and `S.n_configurations()` instead of naming them, so what is edited on purpose is: `PROTOCOL_COUNTS` and its total (113 after 14d: 100 + 1 + 3 + 9) in `test_btcquant_strategies.py`; the pair of literals in `test_the_protocol_document_and_the_code_agree_on_the_version_and_the_trial_count` (`test_btcquant_versions.py`), which also needs the title and the section 5 header of `PROTOCOL.md` (and the family's row in its table) to say the new version and N; the family count in `test_btcquant_calibration.py`; the strategy note `strategies/<id>.md` (a test reads its `Grid:` line and its headings); a logo code in `view.SHORT` (a test fails until it has one); and the toy family of the version tests, which sits at the hypothetical version 1.9 (move it up if a real 1.9 is ever pre-registered). A test that describes an earlier version should pin it (an autouse fixture that sets `C.PROTOCOL_VERSION`, as `test_btcquant_research.py` does). Never change a stored specification hash, account id or old result to get a test green. Tests that read the committed daily-run artifacts must say "at least" and "one per family", not a fixed number: the monitor-count test broke when the `monday` account opened.
- **Give the new family `since="<new version>"` and leave `SCREENED_AGAINST` in `novelty.py` at "1.2".** The queue's novelty screen and its priority order were fixed against the twelve families of 1.2; without the pin the new family joins the comparison set and correlates 1.0 with itself.
- **Small contracts that failed on the first try:** a family's `explain` function must return floats or None (a test checks the type, integers failed it); a fake `evaluate_all` in a test must accept the same keywords as the real one (`families=`); report sentences have to read right for ONE family ("covers the 1 families" got through once); a test that a planted premium is recovered needs a very long synthetic series (the monday test uses 1,050,000 days) or it passes and fails on seed luck.
- **A family that reads a column the synthetic data never varied needs the generator extended first (step 14d, 2026-11-17).** The synthetic candles carried a constant volume of 100, so a volume rule could never fire in any world. The volume series is drawn from a random stream of its own (`seed + 2000`) so that no price, wick or return of any world moved and no earlier calibration figure changed (a test compares the closes with the closes the returns alone produce, and one checks that the volume is uncorrelated with the other two streams); its two numbers were set once from the stored series' volume frequencies (volume only, no price or return); and the `walk()` fixture of the strategy tests needed a volume that changes from day to day, or the look-ahead test cannot see a volume dependence. Check the novelty screen's own code for the rule before writing the family: it decided a detail the queue entry left open (a second surge day inside a hold extends it), and a family that differs from its screen makes the screen's correlation describe another rule.
- **Measure the power before the run and say it in the first lines of the amendment.** The calibration of `volume_surge` found a test that sees even a premium of 6.7 times what a trade costs in only 6% of datasets (A3 on two years of VALIDATION is the limit), and that A1 can fail for the busiest-row settings; the note then says that a rejection carries almost no information. Weigh the next queue entries against that: each adds to N and to the chance of a lucky admission.
- **Compare with the earlier reports before writing "closest" or "best"** (`reports/research_*.md`): six of the first twelve families had failed A3 alone, so a family that fails A3 and A4 with a positive information ratio is a typical result, not a new best (a first draft of the `volume_surge` result said otherwise and was corrected).
- **The file checklist for a whole pass:** `git show --stat 779d9b8` (the pre-registration, pushed before anything was run) and `git show --stat c4c12a0` (the one run and its logs). The order of the steps is in `btcquant/RESEARCH_QUEUE.md`, "How an idea gets from here to a result".
